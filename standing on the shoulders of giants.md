John C. Bogle (1929–2019), commonly known as Jack Bogle, was an American investor, business magnate, and philanthropist who founded The Vanguard Group and created the first retail index fund.
 * Inventor of the Retail Index Fund: In 1975, Bogle launched the First Index Investment Trust (now the Vanguard 500 Index Fund), allowing everyday investors to passively track the S&P 500 at a fraction of the cost of actively managed funds.
 * The "Vanguard Effect": He structured Vanguard so the company is owned by its funds—and therefore by its investors—eliminating outside shareholders and driving investment fees down across the entire financial industry.
 * Investment Philosophy: He championed long-term, low-cost, broadly diversified investing over stock-picking or market timing, famously advising investors: "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack!"
 * Legacy: His approach democratized wealth-building for millions of ordinary savers, inspiring a devoted following of passive investors known as "Bogleheads."


Warren Buffett champions Vanguard's S&P 500 index fund—available as the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO) or the Vanguard 500 Index Fund Admiral Shares (VFIAX).
How Buffett's own money and estate actually interact with it breaks down into three specific details:
 * His Estate Plan (The "90/10" Rule): In his 2013 shareholder letter, Buffett revealed his exact instructions for the cash left to his wife in his will: put 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund ("I suggest Vanguard's") and 10% in short-term government bonds.
 * His Personal Wealth: Almost 99% of Buffett's own net worth is tied up in Berkshire Hathaway stock (which he has pledged to donate to philanthropy) rather than index funds.
 * Berkshire Hathaway's Portfolio: Berkshire Hathaway previously held a small position in VOO (alongside SPY) in its stock portfolio, though it exited both S&P 500 ETFs in late 2024.
Buffett's reasoning mirrors John Bogle's core thesis: over decades, the low fees (0.03% expense ratio for VOO) and broad exposure to America's largest companies beat nearly all high-fee active fund managers.

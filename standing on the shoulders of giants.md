John C. Bogle (1929–2019), commonly known as Jack Bogle, was an American investor, business magnate, and philanthropist who founded The Vanguard Group and created the first retail index fund.
 * Inventor of the Retail Index Fund: In 1975, Bogle launched the First Index Investment Trust (now the Vanguard 500 Index Fund), allowing everyday investors to passively track the S&P 500 at a fraction of the cost of actively managed funds.
 * The "Vanguard Effect": He structured Vanguard so the company is owned by its funds—and therefore by its investors—eliminating outside shareholders and driving investment fees down across the entire financial industry.
 * Investment Philosophy: He championed long-term, low-cost, broadly diversified investing over stock-picking or market timing, famously advising investors: "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack!"
 * Legacy: His approach democratized wealth-building for millions of ordinary savers, inspiring a devoted following of passive investors known as "Bogleheads."


Warren Buffett champions Vanguard's S&P 500 index fund—available as the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO) or the Vanguard 500 Index Fund Admiral Shares (VFIAX).
How Buffett's own money and estate actually interact with it breaks down into three specific details:
 * His Estate Plan (The "90/10" Rule): In his 2013 shareholder letter, Buffett revealed his exact instructions for the cash left to his wife in his will: put 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund ("I suggest Vanguard's") and 10% in short-term government bonds.
 * His Personal Wealth: Almost 99% of Buffett's own net worth is tied up in Berkshire Hathaway stock (which he has pledged to donate to philanthropy) rather than index funds.
 * Berkshire Hathaway's Portfolio: Berkshire Hathaway previously held a small position in VOO (alongside SPY) in its stock portfolio, though it exited both S&P 500 ETFs in late 2024.
Buffett's reasoning mirrors John Bogle's core thesis: over decades, the low fees (0.03% expense ratio for VOO) and broad exposure to America's largest companies beat nearly all high-fee active fund managers.

In 2007, Warren Buffett made a public $1 million charity wager that over a 10-year period (January 1, 2008 – December 31, 2017), a low-cost Vanguard S&P 500 index fund (VFIAX) would outperform a hand-picked portfolio of hedge funds, net of all fees, costs, and expenses.
Ted Seides, co-manager of the asset management firm Protégé Partners, accepted the challenge.
The Rules & Setup
 * Buffett's Pick: Vanguard 500 Index Fund Admiral Shares (VFIAX), charging a tiny annual expense ratio (around 0.04% at the time).
 * Protégé Partners' Pick: Five "funds-of-funds"—investment vehicles that collectively spread capital across more than 200 underlying hedge funds.
 * The Stakes: Each side put up roughly $320,000 into a zero-coupon Treasury bond designed to grow to $1 million by the end of the decade. (When bond yields plunged, the bond's value surged early; they converted it into Berkshire Hathaway stock, which ultimately yielded $2.22 million for Buffett's chosen charity, Girls Inc. of Omaha.)
How It Played Out
The timing initially looked catastrophic for Buffett. In 2008, the Global Financial Crisis hit, and the S&P 500 plunged 37%. All five hedge fund portfolios lost money too, falling an average of 23.9%, giving Protégé Partners an early lead.
However, once the market began recovering in 2009, the S&P 500 beat the hedge fund basket in every single one of the remaining nine years. By the end of 2017, Seides had already conceded defeat months early.

Why Buffett Won: The Math of Fees
Buffett's thesis wasn't that hedge fund managers lack intelligence—it was that high fees create an insurmountable mathematical drag.
 * Double Layer of Fees: Traditional hedge funds typically charge "2 and 20" (2% of assets annually plus 20% of profits). Because Protégé used funds-of-funds, investors paid an additional management layer on top of that—roughly 2.5% in fixed annual fees alone before performance cuts.
 * Buffett's Takeaway: In his 2017 shareholder letter summarizing the bet, Buffett wrote: "Performance comes, performance goes. Fees never falter." Even when the underlying hedge fund managers picked decent stocks, the massive fee structure transferred most of the gains from the investors to the fund managers.

Key Structural Differences Behind the Numbers
 * Concentration vs. Diversification: VFIAX gives you instant exposure to 500 of the largest U.S. companies weighted by market cap—meaning heavy exposure to mega-cap technology (Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, etc.). BRK.B is a single company operating a massive collection of wholly owned businesses (GEICO, BNSF Railway, Berkshire Hathaway Energy) alongside a concentrated public stock portfolio and a massive Treasury cash pile.
 * Dividends & Taxes: VFIAX pays a quarterly dividend (yielding roughly 1.1%), creating annual taxable events in a standard brokerage account. BRK.B pays zero dividends, reinvesting all earnings internally or via share buybacks—meaning you defer capital gains taxes until you sell.
 * Expense Ratio: VFIAX charges a 0.04% annual expense ratio. BRK.B is an individual stock with 0% fund fees.
 * Downside & Volatility: Historically, BRK.B has often acted defensively during market downturns thanks to its steady insurance/utility cash flows and huge cash reserves—for instance, dropping less than VFIAX during the 2020 COVID crash and the 2022 bear market.
